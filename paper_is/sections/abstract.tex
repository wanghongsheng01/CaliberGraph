\begin{abstract}
Execution accuracy (EX), the standard Text-to-SQL metric, accepts a prediction if it returns the gold result on one database. Recent verifiers report that many accepted predictions are not equivalent to the gold query. We study what such counterexample databases prove in three pre-registered stages, on \V{nExc} accepted predictions of ten systems on the BIRD Text-to-SQL benchmark, thirteen further systems and the Spider benchmark. Under one SQLite certifier, budget and admissibility rules, we compare five disprover families. Our join-graph-based disprover, CaliberGraph, certifies more pairs than the bounded verifier SpotIt re-run under our five-second budget, also at the fairer tier of declared schema constraints, although SpotIt models MySQL semantics and would gain from a larger budget. Counts depend on the rules: almost no witness admissible under schema- and format-derived rules satisfies a data profile of the benchmark instance, although searching real sub-databases still certifies two thirds as many pairs. Whether witnesses are evidence is unclear. A large language model and two humans shown facts about the data judged most schema-tier witnesses implausible. In a third round, however, three annotators agreed barely above chance (Fleiss' $\kappa=\V{hvThreeFleissBin}$ on implausible vs.\ other). At the profile tier, our CaliberGraph-R certifies more pairs than random fuzzing on BIRD and the new systems, but not on Spider. Counted as errors, profile-tier witnesses lower EX by \V{ptsCountRMin}--\V{ptsCountRMax} points per system, or by \V{ptsErrRMin}--\V{ptsErrRMax} points if weighted by the model's attributed error rates, an uncertain estimate. We report every pre-registered outcome, including failures, and release all artifacts.
\end{abstract}
