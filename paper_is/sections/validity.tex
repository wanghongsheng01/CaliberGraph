\section{Validity of the Attribution}\label{sec:validity}

This section examines whether the AI attribution of Section~\ref{sec:attrresults} can be trusted. It reports three pre-registered rounds of human annotation, a check with a second model and a post hoc repair test that needs no annotator.

\input{tables/humanai}

\textbf{Primary and secondary evidence.} Our answer to RQ2 rests first on outcomes that are defined mechanically and need no annotator: the certified counts, whether a witness satisfies the data profile $\Pi$ (Section~\ref{sec:rresults}), and whether its difference survives a repair with real values (the annotator-free test below). The attribution of each witness to the classes of Section~\ref{sec:meaningful} is secondary evidence, because human annotators did not reproduce it reliably. The pre-registered hypotheses H6 and H7 rest on the attribution and are reported as registered.

\textbf{Human annotation.} As part of the pre-registered Stage~3, human annotators labeled random samples of witnesses, half from each stage, in three rounds (Table~\ref{tab:humanai}). They worked independently and blind to tool, tier and AI verdict. Their recruitment and consent are described in the Ethics statement at the end of the paper. We write $\kappa_2$ for Cohen's or, for three annotators, Fleiss' $\kappa$ on implausible vs.\ other. In round~1, two annotators saw only the witness and the queries. They agreed with each other on the five labels ($\kappa=\V{hvKappaHH}$). They did not, however, agree with the AI ($\kappa=\V{hvKappaHAmin}$--\V{hvKappaHAmax}). Among the 30 first-stage witnesses, they chose \emph{implausible data} only \V{hvImpHOne} and \V{hvImpHTwo} times, where the AI chose it \V{hvImpAIOne} times. Many of their reasons describe the anomaly the AI cites---e.g., a lookup row \texttt{(3,\textquotesingle{}Bad\textquotesingle{})} where BIRD stores \texttt{(3,\textquotesingle{}Neutral\textquotesingle{})}---but resolve it as an error of one query \emph{on the witness}.

In round~2, every item also showed mechanically generated facts about $D_0$: the \texttt{NULL} counts, uniqueness and frequencies of the witness values of each referenced column. The instructions asked first whether the difference could arise on real data. With these facts, the same annotators agreed on implausibility ($\kappa_2=\V{hvTwoKappaBin}$, CI \V{hvTwoKappaBinLo}--\V{hvTwoKappaBinHi}). They also reproduced the gap between the tiers (\V{hvTwoImpConsOne} of \V{hvTwoConsOne} vs.\ \V{hvTwoImpConsTwo} of \V{hvTwoConsTwo} agreed witnesses, $p=\V{hvTwoP}$). However, they agreed with the AI only moderately ($\kappa_2=\V{hvTwoKappaAIbinMax}$), and they called many more witnesses prediction errors and almost none ambiguous.

Round~3 was pre-registered after an independent pre-submission review asked for more annotators. It gave \V{hvThreeN} new witnesses to three unpaid volunteers, data analysts in e-commerce and social media, together with the same facts and the AI annotator's label definitions verbatim, but without the prescribed order of questions. They agreed with each other barely above chance (Fleiss' $\kappa=\V{hvThreeFleissFive}$, $\kappa_2=\V{hvThreeFleissBin}$, CI \V{hvThreeFleissBinLo} to \V{hvThreeFleissBinHi}). They chose \emph{implausible data} \V{hvThreeImpA}, \V{hvThreeImpB} and \V{hvThreeImpC} times, where the AI chose it \V{hvThreeImpAI} times. \V{hvThreeNoMaj} items have no majority label. The majority agreed with the AI with $\kappa_2=\V{hvThreeKappaAIbin}$. It called \V{hvThreeImpOne} of \V{hvThreeMajOne} first-stage and \V{hvThreeImpTwo} of \V{hvThreeMajTwo} second-stage witnesses implausible ($p=\V{hvThreePThree}$). It also saw more prediction errors than the AI at $\mathcal A_R$ (\V{hvThreeErrHumTwo} vs.\ \V{hvThreeErrAITwo}, $p=\V{hvThreePFour}$).

\textbf{Process record of round~3.} We report the round as received, although its process record is imperfect. First, the start and finish times recorded on the sheets are partly impossible: one finish lies after the return, and one start lies before the sheets existed. These times also differ from the authors' later account that all three annotated by hand from 11:00 to 14:00 on the day of distribution, i.e., about 1.2 minutes per item against an estimated three. Second, one annotator gave 24 distinct, mostly generic reasons for 150 items. Third, one sheet passed through an editor that removed tag-like values from two items and turned a URL in a third into a link. All three annotators reported working without AI tools. In a post hoc sensitivity analysis without the annotator with generic reasons, annotator~A, the other two agree on implausibility with $\kappa_2=\V{hvThreeKappaBCbin}$. Their agreement is thus also near chance, so the low agreement is not due to annotator~A alone. Without annotator~A, annotators B and C agree on \V{rtBCcons} items, with a five-label $\kappa$ of \V{rtBCkappaFive}. Their consensus agrees with the AI with $\kappa_2=\V{rtBCkappaAI}$. It calls \V{rtBCImpOne} of \V{rtBCConsOne} first-stage and \V{rtBCImpTwo} of \V{rtBCConsTwo} second-stage witnesses implausible, and sees prediction errors in \V{rtBCErrTwo} of \V{rtBCNTwo} second-stage witnesses, against \V{rtBCErrAITwo} for the AI on the same items. Given R3-1 and this process record, every round-3 estimate in this paper is of limited reliability, including the human error rates used in Section~\ref{sec:rankings}.

By the pre-stated rules, neither the five-label attribution nor the implausibility judgment is reliably reproducible by humans. The AI shares of Section~\ref{sec:attrresults} are one annotator's operational judgment.

\textbf{A second model.} A second model, Claude Sonnet~5.5, given the AI annotator's prompt and data access, agreed with it ($\kappa=\V{cmKappaBin}$ on \V{cmN} witnesses). It also reproduced the stage gap (\V{cmImpOne} vs.\ \V{cmImpTwo} of 100).

\textbf{An annotator-free test.} In this post hoc test, we repaired each first-stage witness locally and re-ran both queries. Each witness received 20 random repairs, which replace the cells that violate the data profile $\Pi$ (Section~\ref{sec:profile}) by real values. The difference vanished on every repair for \V{xVan} of \V{xN} witnesses (\V{xVanPct}\,\%) and survived on some repairs for \V{xSurv}. It vanished for \V{xImpVanPct}\,\% of the witnesses the AI called implausible, but also for \V{xOthVanPct}\,\% of the others. It also vanished for \V{xHumVan} of the \V{xHumRep} repaired first-stage witnesses that both round-2 annotators called implausible. Yet for \V{xVanPairARpct}\,\% of the witnesses whose difference vanished, and for \V{xPairARpct}\,\% of all, CaliberGraph-R or fuzzing-R found an $\mathcal A_R$ witness for the same pair. In these cases, the two queries do differ on realistic data, but the first-stage witness does not show it.

\textbf{What the checks measure.} Without the facts, the round-1 annotators judged the \emph{pair}, that is, which query answers the question. In contrast, the AI, the round-2 annotators and the repair test judge the \emph{witness}, that is, whether this database is evidence of that difference. The three are not independent of our definition. The facts shown in round~2 and the cells the repair replaces both come from $D_0$ and its profile. They therefore test the consistency of an operational notion of realism, not realism itself.
