\subsection{RQ1: Certified witnesses at schema-derived tiers}\label{sec:main}
At the primary tier $\mathcal A_{1f}$, CaliberGraph certifies \V{nCGLonef} of the \V{nExc} EX-correct pairs (\V{pCGLonef}\,\%). As part of the pre-registered Stage~3, we re-ran the original SpotIt locally under the same five-second budget. It claims \V{srClaims} pairs. Of its counterexamples, \V{srLzero} are certified at $\mathcal A_0$ and \V{srLonef} at $\mathcal A_{1f}$. CaliberGraph certifies \V{srOneDiffAbs} more pairs at $\mathcal A_{1f}$ (SP1, 95\,\% CI \V{srOneCI}). At $\mathcal A_0$, it certifies \V{srTwoDiffAbs} more (SP2, CI \V{srTwoCI}), and only \V{srTwoSIonly} pairs have an $\mathcal A_0$ witness from SpotIt alone. $\mathcal A_0$ is the fairer comparison. SpotIt receives no format samples, stops at its first validated rather than first certified counterexample, and models MySQL semantics on upper-cased queries. Part of its loss therefore reflects the mismatch with SQLite rather than its search.

Counted over distinct pairs, the re-run reproduces \V{srRefoundPct}\,\% of the released claims, so the short budget costs about \V{srLossPct}\,\%. Of the distinct pairs, \V{srKilledPct}\,\% exhaust the budget and \V{srErr} end in a solver error. The median time to a counterexample is \V{srMedCE}\,s. As a diagnostic, restoring the literals' case certifies fewer pairs (\V{srRecased} at $\mathcal A_0$). SpotIt's released claims are counted without certification and took up to about 20 minutes per pair. Against these claims, CaliberGraph certifies \V{hOneDiff} more pairs (H1, CI \V{hOneCI}).

Under identical budgets, acceptance tests and format knowledge, ParSEval-L1 certifies \V{nPVLonef} pairs and random fuzzing \V{nFZLonef}. Only \V{vsPVBOnly} and \V{vsFZBOnly} of their pairs, respectively, lack a CaliberGraph witness. CaliberGraph's lead is specific to these tiers. At $\mathcal A_2$, whose LLM-validated constraints none of the three tools uses, random fuzzing certifies more pairs (\V{nFZLtwo} vs.\ \V{nCGLtwo}). Given those constraints, CaliberGraph+SC certifies \V{nCGSCLtwo} pairs against \V{nSIPClaims} claimed by SpotIt+ (H2). In a pre-registered sensitivity check with the official full-column format profiles, CaliberGraph keeps \V{nCGOonef} pairs, ParSEval-L1 \V{nPVOonef}, fuzzing \V{nFZOonef}, and SpotIt, SpotIt+ and CaliberGraph+SC none.

Released verifier counterexamples lose many pairs on re-certification. Of SpotIt's \V{nSIClaims} claims, \V{nSILzero} counterexamples still separate the original queries on SQLite and satisfy the DDL, and \V{nSILonef} are also format-faithful. For SpotIt+, the counts are \V{nSIPLzero} and \V{nSIPLonef} of \V{nSIPClaims}. On development data, the loss at $\mathcal A_0$ was traced mainly to preprocessing, namely upper-cased literals and unmodeled \texttt{NOT NULL} and \texttt{UNIQUE} constraints, rather than to wrong verdicts. The further loss comes from implausible value formats. Multiset equality leaves every count unchanged (Table~\ref{tab:contracts} in the appendix), and the lenient $\mathcal C_{\mathrm{len}}$ keeps \V{nCGLonefLenient} of CaliberGraph's \V{nCGLonef} pairs.

\textbf{Primary and secondary evidence.} We answer RQ2 first with outcomes that are defined mechanically and need no annotator: the certified counts, whether a witness satisfies the data profile $\Pi$ (Section~\ref{sec:rresults}), and whether its difference survives a repair with real values (the annotator-free test below). The attribution of each witness to the classes of Section~\ref{sec:meaningful} is secondary evidence, because human annotators did not reproduce it reliably. The pre-registered hypotheses H6 and H7 rest on the attribution and are reported as registered.

In this post hoc analysis, the two annotation passes agree on \V{attrAgree}\,\% of the \V{attrN} first-stage witnesses ($\kappa=\V{attrKappa}$), and \V{attrAdj} disagreements were adjudicated. For every tool, \V{attrImplMin}--\V{attrImplMax}\,\% of the sampled witnesses separate the queries only on implausible data, and only \V{attrErrMin}--\V{attrErrMax}\,\% expose an error of the prediction (Fig.~\ref{fig:attribution}). For CaliberGraph, \V{pCGImpl}\,\% of the sampled witnesses are implausible (CI over questions \V{pCGImplClusterCI}). The decisive implausible facts are of few kinds. Some place a \texttt{NULL} in a column that is never \texttt{NULL} in BIRD. Some duplicate what is unique in reality, such as a badge awarded twice or two races of one Grand Prix in a season. Some use values that do not occur, such as a negative round or an atom identifier of the wrong pattern. The others are inconsistent records, such as a school whose county differs between two tables, a bond stored in one direction, or an account without an owner.

CaliberGraph's lead of \V{vsFZDiff} certified pairs over fuzzing does \emph{not} carry over to meaningful witnesses. Weighted by certified counts, CaliberGraph yields an estimated \V{estCGMeaningful} meaningful witnesses \V{estCGMeaningfulCI} and random fuzzing \V{estFZMeaningful} \V{estFZMeaningfulCI}. Nor do CaliberGraph's witnesses expose more estimated prediction errors than fuzzing's (\V{estCGErr} vs.\ \V{estFZErr}). Of the \V{nSIClaims} pairs SpotIt claims to be non-equivalent, \V{nSILzero} can be re-certified, and an estimated \V{estSIErr} of these expose a prediction error. Since attribution judges the returned witness, not the pair, these are lower bounds on errors among certified pairs.

\textbf{Validity of the attribution.} As part of the pre-registered Stage~3, human annotators labeled random samples of witnesses, half from each stage, in three rounds (Table~\ref{tab:humanai}). They worked independently and blind to tool, tier and AI verdict. Their recruitment and consent are described in the Ethics statement at the end of the paper. We write $\kappa_2$ for Cohen's or, for three annotators, Fleiss' $\kappa$ on implausible vs.\ other. In round~1, two annotators saw only the witness and the queries. They agreed with each other on the five labels ($\kappa=\V{hvKappaHH}$). They did not, however, agree with the AI ($\kappa=\V{hvKappaHAmin}$--\V{hvKappaHAmax}). Among the 30 first-stage witnesses, they chose \emph{implausible data} only \V{hvImpHOne} and \V{hvImpHTwo} times, where the AI chose it \V{hvImpAIOne} times. Many of their reasons describe the anomaly the AI cites---e.g., a lookup row \texttt{(3,\textquotesingle{}Bad\textquotesingle{})} where BIRD stores \texttt{(3,\textquotesingle{}Neutral\textquotesingle{})}---but resolve it as an error of one query \emph{on the witness}.

In round~2, every item also showed mechanically generated facts about $D_0$: the \texttt{NULL} counts, uniqueness and frequencies of the witness values of each referenced column. The instructions asked first whether the difference could arise on real data. With these facts, the same annotators agreed on implausibility ($\kappa_2=\V{hvTwoKappaBin}$, CI \V{hvTwoKappaBinLo}--\V{hvTwoKappaBinHi}). They also reproduced the gap between the tiers (\V{hvTwoImpConsOne} of \V{hvTwoConsOne} vs.\ \V{hvTwoImpConsTwo} of \V{hvTwoConsTwo} agreed witnesses, $p=\V{hvTwoP}$). However, they agreed with the AI only moderately ($\kappa_2=\V{hvTwoKappaAIbinMax}$), and they called many more witnesses prediction errors and almost none ambiguous.

Round~3 was pre-registered after an independent pre-submission review asked for more annotators. It gave \V{hvThreeN} new witnesses to three unpaid volunteers, data analysts in e-commerce and social media, together with the same facts and the AI annotator's label definitions verbatim, but without the prescribed order of questions. They agreed with each other barely above chance (Fleiss' $\kappa=\V{hvThreeFleissFive}$, $\kappa_2=\V{hvThreeFleissBin}$, CI \V{hvThreeFleissBinLo} to \V{hvThreeFleissBinHi}). They chose \emph{implausible data} \V{hvThreeImpA}, \V{hvThreeImpB} and \V{hvThreeImpC} times, where the AI chose it \V{hvThreeImpAI} times. \V{hvThreeNoMaj} items have no majority label. The majority agreed with the AI with $\kappa_2=\V{hvThreeKappaAIbin}$. It called \V{hvThreeImpOne} of \V{hvThreeMajOne} first-stage and \V{hvThreeImpTwo} of \V{hvThreeMajTwo} second-stage witnesses implausible ($p=\V{hvThreePThree}$). It also saw more prediction errors than the AI at $\mathcal A_R$ (\V{hvThreeErrHumTwo} vs.\ \V{hvThreeErrAITwo}, $p=\V{hvThreePFour}$).

We report the round as received, although its process record is imperfect. First, the start and finish times recorded on the sheets are partly impossible: one finish lies after the return, and one start lies before the sheets existed. These times also differ from the authors' later account that all three annotated by hand from 11:00 to 14:00 on the day of distribution, i.e., about 1.2 minutes per item against an estimated three. Second, one annotator gave 24 distinct, mostly generic reasons for 150 items. Third, one sheet passed through an editor that removed tag-like values from two items and turned a URL in a third into a link. All three annotators reported working without AI tools. In a post hoc sensitivity analysis without the annotator with generic reasons, annotator~A, the other two agree on implausibility with $\kappa_2=\V{hvThreeKappaBCbin}$. Their agreement is thus also near chance, so the low agreement is not due to annotator~A alone. The artifact reports the other round-3 measures without annotator~A as well. Given R3-1 and this process record, every round-3 estimate in this paper is of limited reliability, including the human error rates used in Section~\ref{sec:rankings}.

By the pre-stated rules, neither the five-label attribution nor the implausibility judgment is reliably reproducible by humans. The AI shares below are one annotator's operational judgment. A second model, Claude Sonnet~5.5, given the AI annotator's prompt and data access, agreed with it ($\kappa=\V{cmKappaBin}$ on \V{cmN} witnesses). It also reproduced the stage gap (\V{cmImpOne} vs.\ \V{cmImpTwo} of 100).

\textbf{An annotator-free test.} In this post hoc test, we repaired each first-stage witness locally and re-ran both queries. Each witness received 20 random repairs, which replace the cells that violate the data profile $\Pi$ (Section~\ref{sec:profile}) by real values. The difference vanished on every repair for \V{xVan} of \V{xN} witnesses (\V{xVanPct}\,\%) and survived on some repairs for \V{xSurv}. It vanished for \V{xImpVanPct}\,\% of the witnesses the AI called implausible, but also for \V{xOthVanPct}\,\% of the others. It also vanished for \V{xHumVan} of the \V{xHumRep} repaired first-stage witnesses that both round-2 annotators called implausible. Yet for \V{xVanPairARpct}\,\% of the witnesses whose difference vanished, and for \V{xPairARpct}\,\% of all, CaliberGraph-R or fuzzing-R found an $\mathcal A_R$ witness for the same pair. In these cases, the two queries do differ on realistic data, but the first-stage witness does not show it.

Without the facts, the round-1 annotators judged the \emph{pair}, that is, which query answers the question. In contrast, the AI, the round-2 annotators and the repair test judge the \emph{witness}, that is, whether this database is evidence of that difference. The three are not independent of our definition. The facts shown in round~2 and the cells the repair replaces both come from $D_0$ and its profile. They therefore test the consistency of an operational notion of realism, not realism itself.

None of CaliberGraph's \V{nCGLonef} first-stage witnesses satisfies the full data profile, and \V{nCGAtRq} satisfy it on the referenced columns. Column-level mined constraints do not close the gap. CaliberGraph+SC certifies \V{nCGSCLtwo} pairs at $\mathcal A_2$ but only \V{nCGSCAtR} at $\mathcal A_R$. None of SpotIt+'s \V{nSIPLtwo} $\mathcal A_2$ counterexamples reaches $\mathcal A_R$, and \V{pSIPImpl}\,\% of SpotIt+'s sampled witnesses are implausible.

CaliberGraph-R certifies \V{nCGRAtR} EX-correct pairs at $\mathcal A_R$ (\V{pCGRAtR}\,\%). Random fuzzing-R, with the same data access, repair and acceptance, certifies \V{nFZRAtR} (difference \V{tCGRvsFZRRDiff}, CI \V{tCGRvsFZRRCI}). On a subsample of \V{nSubR} pairs, ParSEval-R certifies \V{ablRPV} and CaliberGraph-R \V{ablRFull}. H5 is supported. The lead holds on the schemas held out in the first stage, which informed the tier's design (\V{rateCGRHeld} vs.\ \V{rateFZRHeld}\,\% of EX-correct pairs). It holds as well on the other schemas (\V{rateCGROther} vs.\ \V{rateFZROther}\,\%).

\textbf{Why schema-tier witnesses fail the profile.} This analysis is post hoc. On the columns the queries reference, 72--86\,\% of the first-stage witnesses use a value outside the column's observed domain, such as an unseen category, shape or range (Table~\ref{tab:profile}). In addition, 30--70\,\% put a \texttt{NULL} into one of the 420 columns that are never \texttt{NULL} in BIRD, and 21--56\,\% break a value dependency (e.g., a county code paired with another county's name). Half of ParSEval's minimal instances also duplicate a unique column combination. Every constraint class is violated by some tool's witnesses.

Certified counts are insensitive to the categorical thresholds of the profile. We re-certified the stored witnesses with halved and with doubled thresholds. With halved thresholds, CaliberGraph-R keeps \V{thrCGRLooser} of its \V{thrCGRFrozen} distinct certified pairs and fuzzing-R \V{thrFZRLooser} of its \V{thrFZRFrozen}. With doubled thresholds, they keep \V{thrCGRStricter} and \V{thrFZRStricter}, respectively. Neither setting admits any of CaliberGraph's first-stage witnesses. The check varies only the categorical thresholds. The never-\texttt{NULL} rule and the dependency classes were not varied.

\textbf{What the tier changes.} \V{pCGRImpl}\,\% of CaliberGraph-R's sampled witnesses rely on implausible data, against \V{pCGImpl}\,\% of CaliberGraph's $\mathcal A_{1f}$ witnesses (H7, one-sided $p=\V{hSevenP}$). Over questions, the CI for CaliberGraph-R is \V{pCGRImplClusterCI}, and the test gives $p\V{hSevenClusterP}$. Pass agreement is \V{attrRAgree}\,\% ($\kappa=\V{attrRKappa}$). The drop holds on the first-stage held-out schemas (\V{implCGRHeld} vs.\ \V{implCGHeld}). It also holds on the others (\V{implCGROther} vs.\ \V{implCGOther}). The drop is not annotator drift: fifty first-stage witnesses re-annotated in the same batch received the same verdict in \V{calibSame} cases. The rise in the meaningful share is due to the tier, not to the search, since random fuzzing-R reaches \V{pFZRMeaningful}\,\%. However, H7 is close to circular. The annotator could query $D_0$ to check whether the data of a witness occur there, and most CaliberGraph-R witnesses come from its real sub-database phase (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}), so a judge with this access will rarely call them implausible. H7 therefore confirms that the tier works as constructed; it does not show independently that the tier's witnesses are meaningful. The evidence for the tier that does not depend on such a judge is mechanical: schema-tier witnesses violate the profile, and repairs with real values remove many of their differences (Section~\ref{sec:attrresults}).

CaliberGraph-R yields an estimated \V{estCGRMeaningful} meaningful witnesses, against \V{estFZRMeaningful} for fuzzing-R. The difference of \V{hSixDiff} was significant under the pre-registered parametric bootstrap (CI \V{hSixCI}, $p=\V{hSixP}$). However, it was not robustly so under a bootstrap over questions (CI \V{hSixClusterCI}, one-sided $p=\V{hSixClusterP}$). We therefore regard H6 as only weakly supported. Post hoc, fuzzing-R's witnesses show a prediction error more often (estimated \V{estFZRErr} against \V{estCGRErr}). As its certified pairs are almost a subset of CaliberGraph-R's, with \V{tCGRvsFZRRBOnly} exceptions, the difference lies in \emph{which} witness is returned for a pair.

\textbf{A limitation of the tier.} A sub-database may omit the entity a question names, so that one query returns no row and the other a row of \texttt{NULL}s. This happens for \V{nEmptyNullCGR} of CaliberGraph-R's and \V{nEmptyNullFZR} of fuzzing-R's 100 sampled witnesses, all but one of which the attribution counts as meaningful. Under $\mathcal C_{\mathrm{len}}$, which equates the two results, CaliberGraph-R and fuzzing-R certify \V{nCGRAtRClen} and \V{nFZRAtRClen} pairs.

\textbf{A meaningful data-grounded witness.} The following witness exposes a gold query that contradicts its own evidence. For ``How many cards available in paper have a positive starting maximum hand size?'', the evidence defines availability as \texttt{LIKE \textquotesingle{}\%paper\%\textquotesingle{}}, but the gold query tests \texttt{availability = \textquotesingle{}paper\textquotesingle{}}. CaliberGraph-R returns three real cards, one with the observed value \texttt{\textquotesingle{}arena,mtgo,paper\textquotesingle{}}. On these cards, the gold query counts one card and the prediction two.

\textbf{Recall on known positives.} CaliberGraph finds an $\mathcal A_{1f}$ witness for \V{rCGLonef}\,\% of the \V{nExi} known positives, against \V{rPVLonef}\,\% for ParSEval-L1, \V{rFZLonef}\,\% for fuzzing and \V{rSI}\,\% claimed by SpotIt (H3). At $\mathcal A_R$, the figure is a sanity check rather than an estimate, because $D_0$ itself separates every known positive and satisfies its profile up to the approximate cross-key rules. CaliberGraph-R finds a witness for \V{rCGRAtR}\,\% of them and fuzzing-R for \V{rFZRAtR}\,\%. No recall estimate exists for EX-correct pairs, on which a missed difference is invisible.

In a post hoc analysis, we weight each system's certified share by the attributed prediction-error rate of the corresponding tool, CaliberGraph at $\mathcal A_{1f}$ and CaliberGraph-R at $\mathcal A_R$. The estimates also depend on who attributes. As pre-registered, we apply the round-3 human majority's error rates, although they are of limited reliability given R3-1. The majority attributes \V{humThreeErrPctOnef}\,\% of schema-tier witnesses to prediction errors (CI \V{humThreeErrPctOnefLo}--\V{humThreeErrPctOnefHi}). For $\mathcal A_R$ witnesses, the rate is \V{humThreeErrPctR}\,\% (CI \V{humThreeErrPctRLo}--\V{humThreeErrPctRHi}). On the same items, the AI's rates are \V{aiThreeErrPctOnef} and \V{aiThreeErrPctR}\,\%. Counting items without a majority as non-errors gives human rates of \V{humThreeErrAllPctOnef} and \V{humThreeErrAllPctR}\,\%. With the human rates, the weighting gives \V{ptsHumThreeOnefMin}--\V{ptsHumThreeOnefMax} points at $\mathcal A_{1f}$ and \V{ptsHumThreeRMin}--\V{ptsHumThreeRMax} at $\mathcal A_R$ (\V{ptsHumThreeRMinLo}--\V{ptsHumThreeRMaxHi} over the interval), where counting every witness gives \V{ptsCountRMin}--\V{ptsCountRMax}.

Data grounding thus changes what witnesses mean much more than how many errors they expose. The estimated prediction errors are similar at both tiers, \V{estCGErr} for CaliberGraph and \V{estCGRErr} for CaliberGraph-R. What the remaining witnesses show is uncertain. By the AI attribution, 58--64\,\% of the sampled $\mathcal A_R$ witnesses point to gold-query defects or ambiguities, some on sub-databases without the entity the question names. In contrast, every human round attributes more $\mathcal A_R$ witnesses to prediction errors (Table~\ref{tab:humanai}).

In the first stage, join-graph completion is the only component whose removal lowers the count significantly (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). Without it, \V{hFourNoJoinFullOnly} of \V{subFull} pairs are lost and none are gained (Holm-adjusted $p=0.004$). Removing anchoring, isolation or the SQLite value domain changes the count by at most two pairs, and the median time to a witness stays at 6--8\,ms. \textbf{H4 is not supported.} The differences these components target are also reached by the other phases and by the mutation loop, which applies the same operators at random. Their benefit on development pairs therefore did not carry over. In the second stage, the real sub-database phase accounts for most witnesses. Without this phase, the count is \V{ablRNoReal} ($p=\V{ablRNoRealP}$), which is fewer than fuzzing-R's. Anchored retrieval and join completion also contribute ($p=\V{ablRNoAnchorP}$ and $p=\V{ablRNoJoinP}$, respectively). Constrained synthesis, in contrast, adds few witnesses ($p=\V{ablRNoSynP}$).

\textbf{Cost.} When CaliberGraph finds an $\mathcal A_{1f}$ witness, the median search time is \V{tCGMedian}\,s, and \V{tCGWithinOne}\,\% of these witnesses are found within 1\,s (Fig.~\ref{fig:time}). ParSEval-L1 needs \V{tPVMedian}\,s, and CaliberGraph-R needs \V{tCGRRMedian}\,s at $\mathcal A_R$, including its queries to $D_0$. However, most distinct pairs end without a witness after the full budget, so the budget dominates the cost. Auditing the accepted test predictions of one system alone, about 800, with CaliberGraph-R takes \V{costSysMean} core-hours on average (range \V{costSysMin}--\V{costSysMax}). Auditing all ten systems takes 6.9 core-hours when identical predictions are shared. Doubling the budget leaves CaliberGraph's count unchanged and raises ParSEval-L1's from \V{subPVFive} to \V{subPVTen}. In a post hoc check on the same subsample at $\mathcal A_R$, doubling changes CaliberGraph-R's count from 195 to 193 and fuzzing-R's from 151 to 153. Neither stage is therefore budget-bound. Mining the profile is a one-time cost per benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:profile}). Attribution, which F2 makes necessary (Table~\ref{tab:findings}), is a manual or model-assisted cost of a sample per tool and is not included.

\textbf{Root causes and benchmark issues.} We analyzed a sample of 100 first-stage CaliberGraph witnesses, AI-annotated in two passes (Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} in the appendix). In this sample, \texttt{NULL} handling, filter predicates and join paths cause two thirds of the differences, with 30\,\%, 18\,\% and 17\,\%, respectively. They are followed by ordering and \texttt{LIMIT} ties with 12\,\%, duplicates with 9\,\% and aggregates over empty input with 8\,\%.

Across all attribution samples, with 77 items from schema-tier samples and 46 from $\mathcal A_R$ samples, \V{nIssues} distinct test questions received a witness that the AI annotators judged to expose a gold-query defect or an ambiguous question. Of these, \V{nIssuesGold} concern gold-query defects and \V{nIssuesAmb} ambiguous questions. Of all flagged questions, \V{nIssuesFragile} are fragile: their witness lacks the entity the question names, or the two results differ only in rounding or letter case. The remaining \V{nIssuesRobust} comprise \V{nIssuesRobustGold} gold defects and \V{nIssuesRobustAmb} ambiguities. The gold defects are \emph{latent}: on $D_0$, the gold query returns the accepted answer, but on the witness it answers the question wrongly. Recurrent kinds are 11 wrong join paths or attributes and 8 filters that contradict the question's evidence, which use inclusive \texttt{BETWEEN} for strict bounds, equality for \texttt{LIKE} or case-sensitive matches. Further recurrent kinds are 7 aggregates over an empty selection that return \texttt{NULL} where a count of zero is meant, 7 counts of rows or names where entities are counted, and 3 averages that count \texttt{NULL}s. About half of the ambiguities concern ties. We release the list, with one witness, the annotators' reasons and these flags per question, as a candidate list for maintainers rather than a verdict.

In a pre-registered comparison, the list overlaps only weakly with the corrected annotations of Jin et al.~\cite{jin2026annotation}. Their Arcwise-Plat set covers \V{arcG} of the questions we flag as gold defects. Of these, \V{arcGk}, or \V{arcGpct}\,\%, received a gold query with a different result on $D_0$, against \V{arcNpct}\,\% of the \V{arcNN} unflagged test questions (one-sided Fisher $p=\V{arcPone}$). Our ambiguity flags do not predict their edits (\V{arcApct}\,\% vs.\ \V{arcANpct}\,\%). Post hoc, counting textual corrections of the gold query gives a rate of \V{arcGtextPct}\,\% against \V{arcNtextPct}\,\%. Our witnesses can expose such corrections although the corrected query returns the same result on $D_0$. \V{arcGuntouched} flagged gold defects and \V{arcAuntouched} flagged ambiguities were left entirely unchanged by Arcwise-Plat.


\subsection{RQ6: Replication on Spider and on new systems}\label{sec:replication}
In Stage~3, we re-ran the frozen code on Spider~1.0 dev~\cite{yu2018spider}, which no design decision had seen. The data comprise 20 databases with \V{spTables} tables and \V{spRows}\,k rows, 1,034 questions, and six public prediction sets: DAIL-SQL~\cite{gao2024dailsql} with and without self-consistency, Graphix-T5~\cite{li2023graphix}, DIN-SQL~\cite{pourreza2023dinsql}, and GPT-4 few- and zero-shot. This yields \V{spN} EX-correct pairs. We exclude 117 pairs whose gold query fails on the Spider database.

The pattern of the tiers replicates. CaliberGraph certifies \V{spCG} pairs at $\mathcal A_{1f}$ but \V{spCGR} at $\mathcal A_R$, and fuzzing certifies \V{spFZ} and \V{spFZR}. The search does not replicate, and the pre-registered S1 is not supported. CaliberGraph-R and fuzzing-R certify \V{spRCG} and \V{spRFZ} pairs at $\mathcal A_R$, with \V{spSOneA} vs.\ \V{spSOneB} discordant pairs (one-sided $p=\V{spSOneP}$, CI over questions \V{spSOneLo} to \V{spSOneHi}). They have equal recall on known positives (\V{spRecCGR} vs.\ \V{spRecFZR}\,\%).

The attribution, in two passes with $\kappa=\V{spKappa}$, finds \V{spImpCG} of \V{spNCG} sampled CaliberGraph witnesses implausible at $\mathcal A_{1f}$, against \V{spImpCGR} of \V{spNCGR} CaliberGraph-R witnesses at $\mathcal A_R$. This difference is not significant after Holm correction over the Stage-3 family (one-sided Fisher $p=\V{spSTwoP}$). None of \V{spNFZR} fuzzing-R witnesses is implausible. The estimated meaningful $\mathcal A_R$ witnesses are \V{spEstCGR} for CaliberGraph-R and \V{spEstFZR} for fuzzing-R. Pre-registered S3 is reversed: the difference has CI \V{spSThreeLo} to \V{spSThreeHi}, in fuzzing-R's favor. Post hoc, we found that almost all implausible CaliberGraph-R witnesses duplicate a real entity under a fresh key, such as two identical teachers. The Spider profile does not forbid this. On small tables, our miner keeps only \V{spUcc} unique column combinations, against \V{bdUcc} for BIRD. Moreover, \V{spCatShare}\,\% of the columns are categorical, against \V{bdCatShare}\,\% for BIRD. As a result, every admissible witness is close to a real sub-database, and random real rows suffice.

On BIRD, the result of the search does replicate on systems the study had not seen. We use the thirteen public prediction sets of BIRD Mini-Dev~\cite{birdminidev,li2023bird} (GPT-3.5/4/4o, Llama-3, Mixtral, Phi-3) and map them to BIRD questions by the released question order. For this mapping, every mapped database matched, and the string literals of 88--93\,\% of predictions occur in the mapped question, evidence or gold query, against at most 15\,\% at neighboring positions. We restrict the analysis to held-out test questions and to (question, prediction) pairs not seen before. On these, CaliberGraph-R certifies \V{mdCGR} of \V{mdN} new EX-correct pairs at $\mathcal A_R$ and fuzzing-R \V{mdFZR}, with \V{mdA} vs.\ \V{mdB} discordant pairs (CI over questions \V{mdLo}--\V{mdHi}, Holm-adjusted $p$ \V{holmEOne}). Of \V{mdAttrN} sampled CaliberGraph-R witnesses, \V{mdImp} were attributed to implausible data, close to the second-stage share (\V{pCGRImpl}\,\%), and \V{mdErr} to a prediction error.
