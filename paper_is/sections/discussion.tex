\textbf{Lessons.} Table~\ref{tab:findings} maps each finding to a recommendation. The common thread is that a counterexample is a database and should be judged as data: certified on the benchmark's engine with the original query strings, checked against the benchmark's own profile, and released so that others can attribute it. Meaningful witnesses are feedback on the benchmark as well as on the systems: by the AI attribution, 58--64\,\% of the sampled data-grounded witnesses expose latent gold-query defects or ambiguous questions, against 11--22\,\% that expose prediction errors; human annotators see more prediction errors (\V{humThreeErrPctR}\,\% by the round-3 majority), but agree too little with each other for a reliable estimate. No correction of system scores can fix gold defects.

\textbf{Disproving is not proving.} A missing witness says nothing; accuracy corrected with witnesses is an upper bound on semantic accuracy, and error-weighted corrections are lower bounds on the over-statement if the labels are unbiased. Bounded verifiers can also report the absence of counterexamples up to a bound, which our tools cannot.

\textbf{What ``implausible'' means.} The profile is mined from one instance, so it can be too strict (a plausible but unobserved value is admitted only through range and shape rules) and too weak: annotators still found conditional constraints it does not express (a badge awarded once while others repeat), which conditional-dependency and denial-constraint discovery could capture~\cite{fan2011cfd,chu2013dc}. ``Implausible'' combines absence from the instance with a judgement about the domain, on which annotators disagree (Section~\ref{sec:attrresults}); a live football database may well contain unplayed fixtures without scores (Fig.~\ref{fig:example}); conversely, a prediction that is right only because a column happens to hold no \texttt{NULL} is fragile under updates, so an implausible witness says that the benchmark, not the domain, cannot separate the queries. Sub-databases can also omit the entity a question names (Section~\ref{sec:rresults}). Some differences rely on behaviour SQL leaves unspecified, such as which tied row \texttt{LIMIT} keeps; BIRD's EX is computed with SQLite, so we count them, and attribution classifies most as ambiguities.

\textbf{AI-performed annotation.} All attribution and root-cause labels were produced by the AI model that also implemented the tools, in two independent passes plus adjudication; agreement between passes measures consistency, not correctness. The first author's earlier checks of two samples of 30 witnesses (agreement 26--28 of 30) were not blind: the AI verdicts were printed on the same sheet. A second model reproduces the labels (Section~\ref{sec:attrresults}), but both share a vendor and the same data access. The human annotators of rounds 1--2, two data analysts familiar with SQL who volunteered, labelled independently. The pre-registered blind check failed in its first round, in which they had no access to $D_0$; in a second, separately pre-registered round with facts from $D_0$ they agreed with each other and reproduced the difference between the tiers, but agreed with the AI only moderately. The two round-2 sheets contained a few manual edits outside the label fields, which the authors first attributed to an editing tool (end-of-file markers, two altered witness cells in one item and a shortened fact line in another; excluding the two items changes no outcome). In round~3, three further unpaid analysts agreed barely above chance, and its process record has the inconsistencies listed in Section~\ref{sec:attrresults}; the authors state that all three rounds were annotated by hand, and the round-3 annotators reported working without AI tools. All sheets are released as received, and the AI's estimated shares carry this uncertainty. Blinding to the tier is imperfect, since synthetic values (\texttt{'x1'}, \texttt{-1}) are recognisable. All annotations, reasons, witnesses and all human sheets are released.

\textbf{Baselines.} SpotIt was re-run from its released code under our budget (Section~\ref{sec:main}); its bounded solver often exhausts five seconds, so a larger budget would favour it, and its released claims, obtained with larger budgets, are reported as well. SpotIt+ is used through its released outputs, since its constraint mining needs an LLM API unavailable in our environment. ParSEval-R certifies nothing at $\mathcal A_R$ and is not a fair representative of symbolic generation there. The frozen first-stage fuzzing baseline accepted the first differing database without consulting the certifier, unlike every other tool; we re-ran exactly the affected pairs (132 distinct EX-correct, 259 EX-incorrect) with certifier-in-the-loop acceptance and the same seeds and report the corrected counts; its attribution sample was drawn before the correction.

\textbf{Deviations from the pre-registrations.} (i)~The attribution of first-stage witnesses was added after the first test run; it replaced a pre-registered residual audit of undisproved pairs. (ii)~The pre-registered manual spot check of rule-based root-cause labels was replaced by two AI passes after an AI check found the rule-based labels imprecise; no human check of root causes was made. (iii)~Before the second freeze, the definition of $\mathcal A_R$ in the second pre-registration was revised on development data: the observed-value condition was restricted to categorical columns, measure columns were excluded from unique column combinations, and the value-dependency class was added; the record of this revision was written after the second test run, but the changed files are covered by the freeze hashes. (iv)~The second stage was designed after seeing first-stage test attributions (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}). (v)~Stage~3 (Section~\ref{sec:replication}, Section~\ref{sec:attrresults}) was pre-registered before any of its data were read (the round-1 sheets were complete but unread); the repair test was added after the first human round (post hoc), its procedure was changed once after a 12-item smoke run, a build fix for two non-UTF-8 cells of one Spider database preceded all Spider runs, and part of one run shared the machine with analysis jobs; the SpotIt re-run ran in resumed chunks of at most 105 minutes, each pair once. (vi)~Stages 1--3 are timestamped by commits of our development repository, not by a public registry (the later pre-registrations by the public repository); the released snapshot lets anyone verify the frozen method and baseline code against the recorded hashes (six analysis-only scripts changed after the freezes are listed), and the full commit history can be made available to the editors. The complete list of amendments, with timestamps, is in the artifact repository.

\textbf{Generalisation.} All results concern SQLite, BIRD and Spider~1.0. The systems answer the same questions, which our intervals account for by resampling questions. The tier was conceived after first-stage test attributions had been seen; its only test on databases no design decision had seen, Spider, did not replicate CaliberGraph-R's lead, which replicated only on new systems over the BIRD databases. Spider also shows that a profile mined from small instances is less informative; larger enterprise schemas and other engines remain to be studied.
